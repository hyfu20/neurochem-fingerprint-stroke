# Results

## Study Population

A total of 3,582 patients with acute ischemic stroke were included in the analysis (mean age 63.1 ± 11.3 years; 66.4% male). Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The median NIHSS at admission was 7 (IQR 6–9), and the median TLV was 2.5 mL (IQR 0.9–11.0). At discharge, 60.9% of patients had favorable outcomes (mRS 0–2), 37.4% had moderate disability (mRS 3–4), and 1.7% had poor outcomes (mRS 5–6). Follow-up rates were 98.5% at 3 months, 98.5% at 6 months, and 97.2% at 12 months. Neurotransmitter load data were complete for all 17 pathways. Inflammatory biomarkers were available for 73.7% (IL-6) and 71.5% (hsCRP) of patients; RMSSD data were available for 53% of patients. Missingness bias testing confirmed no significant baseline differences between patients with and without each assessment (all P > 0.05).

## Neurochemical Disruption Dominates Acute, but Not Long-Term, Functional Outcomes

Ordinal logistic regression with hierarchical sensitivity analysis (Koch residual method) identified six neurotransmitter pathways that robustly predicted functional disability at discharge (Table 2, Fig. 1). To address the extreme collinearity between neurotransmitter loads and TLV (r ≈ 0.99, VIF > 700), we applied the Koch residual approach: Resid_NT = Load_NT − β × TLV, yielding orthogonalized predictors (VIF ≈ 1). In the fully adjusted model (Model C: Resid_NT + TLV + NIHSS + Age + Sex), six neurotransmitter systems achieved significance at P < 10⁻⁴ with global FDR q < 0.05 (Table 2). The strongest associations were driven by the noradrenaline transporter (NAT: OR = 1.284 per 1-SD increase, 95% CI 1.19–1.39, P = 1.88 × 10⁻¹⁰) and nicotinic cholinergic receptor (α4β2: OR = 1.262 per 1-SD, 95% CI 1.17–1.36, P = 1.37 × 10⁻⁹), alongside serotonergic (5-HT6), dopaminergic (DAT), vesicular cholinergic (VAChT), and serotonin transporter (5-HTT) systems. These acute effects remained robust across all adjustment levels (Models A–C) and survived additional control for physical corticospinal tract disconnection (Model D, OR attenuation 14–28%; Fig. 2), indicating that neurochemical signatures provide prognostic information independent of macroscopic anatomical disruption.

Spatial null modeling (1,000-iteration spin tests) confirmed chemoarchitectural specificity for the cholinergic pathway atlas (P_spin = 0.001), with NAT approaching significance (P_spin = 0.074). The remaining systems did not reach significance in the spin test, indicating that their individual prognostic effects may partly reflect spatial co-localization with common infarct territories rather than system-specific vulnerability. Of note, this does not invalidate the NT × inflammation interaction effects (Fig. 3), which operate through a distinct statistical mechanism (effect modification) and are not subject to the spatial autocorrelation concern addressed by spin tests.

Notably, this predictive power underwent a profound temporal decay. When the same TLV-orthogonalized neurochemical fingerprints were regressed against 12-month mRS with full covariate adjustment, no individual neurotransmitter system retained independent prognostic significance (all FDR q > 0.05; Supplementary Fig. S2). This temporal dissociation indicates that acute neurochemical disruption and long-term recovery may be governed by distinct pathophysiological mechanisms, explored further in the neuro-immune analyses below.

## Neurotransmitter Effects Survive Corticospinal Tract Control

To determine whether the neurochemical signal was merely a spatial surrogate for physical motor tract disconnection, we extracted the overlap volume between each patient's lesion and the bilateral CST from the JHU white matter atlas (Mori 2008; Hua 2008) (CST_Load; 53% of patients had CST involvement, median overlap = 285 mm³). CST_Load was moderately correlated with TLV (Spearman r = 0.486), indicating that CST involvement and TLV capture partially independent anatomical information.

CST_Load itself independently predicted outcome in the fully adjusted model (OR = 1.220, P = 5.55 × 10⁻⁶). When CST_Load was incorporated as an additional covariate (Model D), 12 of 17 neurotransmitter variables retained significance after local Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction within the 17-NT family (qₙₜ < 0.05), corresponding to the same 12 variables that survived global FDR correction in the non-CST-controlled Model C (Table 3, Fig. 2). For the top predictors, OR attenuation ranged from 14% to 28% (e.g., NAT: OR 1.284 → 1.243, ΔOR = −14.5%; α4β2: OR 1.262 → 1.217, ΔOR = −17.1%), indicating that over 80% of the neurochemical prognostic effect was independent of physical corticospinal tract disruption.

This dissociation demonstrates that neurotransmitter disconnectome metrics capture a prognostically distinct dimension of stroke injury beyond classical motor tract disconnection.

## Neuro-Immune Interaction: A Joint Dual-Burden Phenotype

The dissociation between significant acute prognostic effects of neurochemical disconnection (Fig. 2) and the loss of independent signal at 12 months (Supplementary Fig. S2) raised the possibility that long-term prognostic relevance of neurotransmitter disruption may be conditional on systemic inflammatory status, that is, specific neurotransmitter hubs may behave as conditional risk modifiers whose disruption becomes prognostically consequential primarily in patients with concurrent inflammatory activation. To test this hypothesis, we performed FDR-corrected interaction analyses between all 17 NT disconnection variables and two complementary baseline inflammatory biomarkers, IL-6 and hsCRP (Fig. 3A).

Of 34 NT × inflammation pairs tested, 7 (20.6%) reached global FDR-corrected significance (q < 0.05; full pairwise results in Table 4). The signals fell into two functional clusters. The first comprised systems previously implicated in central autonomic immunomodulation: the medial cholinergic pathway × hsCRP (q = 0.011), VAChT × IL-6 (q = 0.019), and NAT × IL-6 (q = 0.046). The second was a dopaminergic-serotonergic cluster: DAT × IL-6 (q = 0.011), 5-HT6 × IL-6 (q = 0.024), D2 × IL-6 (q = 0.037), and D1 × IL-6 (q = 0.048). Of note, 6 of the 7 hits involved IL-6 as the inflammatory partner, with the medial cholinergic pathway × hsCRP interaction providing convergent evidence from a biologically independent inflammatory marker.

Departure from additivity was assessed for all seven globally-FDR-significant pairs by partitioning patients into four cells defined by median splits of TLV-orthogonalized NT damage (Resid_NT) and the inflammatory marker (low/low [LL, reference], low/high [LH], high/low [HL], high/high [HH]; Fig. 3B; Table_S4_AdditiveInteraction_AllSigPairs.csv). For the most significant pair (medial cholinergic pathway × hsCRP, N = 2,483; q_global = 0.011), the HH cell carried the highest absolute risk of 12-month poor outcome (adjusted OR_HH = 1.77 [vs. LL reference]; OR_LH = 1.55; OR_HL = 1.27), but on the additive-OR scale the joint risk did not significantly exceed the sum of the two single-hit effects (RERI = −0.05 [95% CI −0.67, +0.51]; AP = −0.03; Synergy index S = 0.94; LL/LH/HL/HH cell-level counts, Wilson 95% CIs and OR_vs_LL in Table_S3_AdditiveInteraction_summary.csv); concurrently, the multiplicative-scale 1-df likelihood-ratio test was not significant (χ²₁ = 0.25, P = 0.61; Fig. 3B), reflecting an approximately additive accumulation of risk on the absolute scale (sub-multiplicative on the OR scale). The remaining six significant pairs (DAT/VAChT/5-HT6/D2/NAT/D1 × IL-6) showed the same qualitative pattern, with all seven RERI confidence intervals crossing zero (Table_S4_AdditiveInteraction_AllSigPairs.csv). Together, these findings indicate that the seven globally-FDR-significant interactions identify high-risk subgroups via the co-occurrence of dual neurochemical-inflammatory burden, hereafter the "dual-burden" phenotype, rather than super-additive synergy on the absolute-risk scale. To visualize this pattern on the continuous inflammation scale and anchor the subsequent prediction analyses on the same cholinergic–inflammation axis, we additionally fit covariate-adjusted simple-slope models within tertiles of TLV-orthogonalized CHA damage (Resid_human_CHA; Fig. 3B, right panel): the inflammation slope was steepest in the Low-damage tertile and progressively attenuated with increasing CHA damage (per-tertile slopes and P-values in Table_S5_SimpleSlope_CHA_IL6.csv), confirming the sub-multiplicative pattern on the continuous scale. To quantify the clinical relevance of this joint phenotype, we constructed nested prediction models with a clinical base (TLV + NIHSS + Age + Sex; AUC = 0.718). Adding NT loads alone provided no incremental value (ΔAUC = +0.000 [−0.001, +0.003], P = 0.62; continuous NRI = +0.027 [−0.082, +0.340], P = 0.39), indicating that neurochemical disconnection metrics are not standalone predictors. However, incorporating the cholinergic–IL-6 axis as an *a priori* dichotomous dual-burden phenotype (high-CHA × high-IL-6 vs. reference; selected on the basis of the established cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway literature) significantly improved both discrimination and reclassification (ΔAUC = +0.010 [+0.004, +0.020], P < 0.001; continuous NRI = +0.351 [+0.082, +0.434], P = 0.002; IDI = +0.009 [+0.003, +0.017], P < 0.001; Fig. 3C), with adequate calibration (Fig. 3D).

As a sensitivity check on the effect-modification interpretation of the NT × IL-6 interactions, bootstrap mediation analysis (5,000 iterations) controlling for TLV, NIHSS, Age, and Sex was performed for all 17 neurotransmitter systems (Supplementary Fig. S5). The indirect effect through baseline IL-6 was non-significant (95% percentile bootstrap CI crossing zero) for the great majority of systems, including the cholinergic projection systems (human_CHA, VAChT) and noradrenergic NAT, indicating that the joint NT × IL-6 prognostic signal operates predominantly through effect modification rather than a uniform causal-mediation pathway.

As an independent longitudinal corroboration of the effect-modification reading, we exploited the CNSR-III protocol-defined hsCRP repeat measurements at 3 months (M03) and 12 months (M12) in the sub-cohort with paired baseline + 3-month hsCRP and 12-month mRS (N = 1,334; three-time-point sub-cohort N = 820; Supplementary Fig. S7). At the population level, hsCRP declined from a median of 1.98 mg/L at baseline to 1.23 mg/L at M03 and 0.81 mg/L at M12. Stratifying by Resid_human_CHA tertile (Supplementary Fig. S7A) revealed virtually superimposable trajectories across the low-, mid- and high-damage tertiles (BSL medians 2.06, 1.50, 2.15 mg/L; M03 medians 1.25, 1.22, 1.25 mg/L; M12 medians 0.78, 0.80, 0.87 mg/L), with the highest-damage tertile (T3) showing the steepest, rather than the slowest, monthly log-hsCRP resolution slope (median per-month slope T1 = −0.05, T3 = −0.08; Kruskal–Wallis P = 0.004; Spearman ρ(Resid_CHA, slope) = −0.04; covariate-adjusted OLS β = +0.002 per 1-SD CHA, P = 0.91), directly inconsistent with the prior hypothesis that cholinergic disconnection impairs the resolution of systemic inflammation. A sustained-hsCRP phenotype (M03_hsCRP ≥ 3 mg/L; 23.2% of the sub-cohort) was associated with 12-month poor outcome on the unadjusted scale (27.3% vs. 17.4% in resolvers; χ² P = 1.4 × 10⁻⁴; Supplementary Fig. S7C), but this signal was almost entirely captured by baseline hsCRP and lost statistical significance after adjustment for BSL_hsCRP + TLV + NIHSS + Age + Sex (adjusted OR = 1.29 [95% CI 0.92, 1.81], P = 0.13). Most importantly, the fully adjusted ordinal model retained two independent main effects, z(Resid_CHA) (OR = 1.13, P = 0.029) and z(slope) (OR = 1.16, P = 0.032), but the Resid_CHA × slope interaction term was effectively null (OR = 1.00; LR test χ²₁ = 0.001, P = 0.98), and the corresponding four-cell additive-interaction estimate for Resid_CHA × sustained-hsCRP yielded a sub-additive RERI of −0.63 (95% CI −1.94, +0.32; OR_HH = 1.53 [0.95, 2.43], lower than OR_LH = 1.79 [1.14, 2.82]). A pre-specified sensitivity analysis on the three-time-point sub-cohort (N = 820), substituting the BSL → M12 per-month log-slope for the BSL → M03 slope, gave directionally identical results (covariate-adjusted β(CHA → slope) ≈ 0, P = 0.68; Resid_CHA × slope ordinal interaction OR = 1.05, P = 0.49; four-cell additive RERI = −0.61 [95% CI −2.31, +0.63]; OR_HH = 1.39 vs. OR_LH = 1.79; Supplementary Fig. S7 sensitivity panel; `out/dynamic_inflammation_triplet/`), confirming that the late hsCRP-resolution window (months 3 to 12) shows the same null interaction pattern as the early window (baseline to month 3). Two prognostic axes therefore accumulate without statistical interaction: cholinergic disconnection and impaired inflammation resolution operate as independent risk factors rather than as a synergistic dual-burden cascade, providing an independent longitudinal corroboration of the cross-sectional effect-modification reading.

## Predictive Performance versus Mechanistic Insight

Decision curve analysis demonstrated that the dual-burden model provided modest net clinical benefit over the clinical base model across threshold probabilities 0.10–0.40 (Fig. 4A). However, 10-fold stratified cross-validation across all 17 NT systems revealed that none of the per-NT incremental improvements exceeded ΔAUC = +0.002 in held-out data (top NT: α4β2, ΔAUC = +0.0015; Fig. 4B), consistent with the recognized difficulty of incrementally improving upon NIHSS-based prediction models for long-term stroke outcome. This dissociation between in-sample significance (NRI P = 0.002) and modest cross-validated performance indicates that while the neuro-immune interaction is associated with statistically significant outcome variance, its predictive utility for individual-level prognostication requires external validation in independent cohorts. Accordingly, the subsequent analyses focus on the spatial and clinical interpretation of this interaction rather than its incremental predictive performance.

## Neuro-Immune Vulnerability Atlas (NIVA)

The preceding analyses identified significant NT × inflammation interactions at the pathway level (Fig. 3) but did not reveal where in the brain these neuro-immune interactions converge anatomically. To generate a spatially explicit synthesis, we constructed the NIVA by projecting 14 PET-derived neurotransmitter receptor/transporter density maps (Hansen 2022) onto MNI152 standard space, each weighted by its empirical NT × inflammation interaction effect size as −log₁₀(global FDR q) (capped at 5), with NT systems failing global FDR (q ≥ 0.05) excluded from the composite (zero weight). The composite map was thresholded at the 80th percentile to delineate regions of maximal neuro-immune convergence (Fig. 5A).

The NIVA revealed spatially selective convergence concentrated in subcortical structures, including the basal ganglia (caudate nucleus, putamen), thalamus, and brainstem regions, with additional cortical involvement of medial prefrontal and insular regions. These high-convergence areas overlap with the known terminal fields of the cholinergic and noradrenergic systems (Mesulam 2004; Zaborszky 2008; Aston-Jones & Cohen 2005) that exhibited the strongest pathway-level interaction effects (Fig. 3A), providing spatial convergence between the atlas-derived map and the dual-burden statistical findings.

Three complementary analyses assessed the robustness of the NIVA (Fig. 5B–D). First, split-half cross-validation, in which the full covariate-adjusted OLS NT × inflammation pipeline was independently re-fitted in each randomly assigned half (n = 1,791 per half) and a half-specific NIVA was reconstructed from scratch at raw $P$ < 0.05 per half, yielded strong voxel-wise concordance between the two half-cohort NIVAs (Pearson $r$ = 0.785, $P$ < 1 × 10⁻³⁰⁰; n = 1,896,044 voxels; 3 and 7 NT systems independently met the threshold in Half-1 and Half-2 respectively), indicating that the same anatomical convergence pattern is recoverable from independent subsamples. Second, 200-iteration bootstrap resampling at the full sample size, with the covariate-adjusted interaction pipeline rerun and NT weights re-derived at raw $P$ < 0.05 per replicate, identified two NT systems, cholinergic human_CHA (selection frequency = 89%) and the dopamine transporter DAT (85%), as robustly selected (≥ 80% of replicates; Hansen et al., 2022), with 5HT6 at the borderline threshold (80%) and the remaining 11 systems contributing intermittently, confirming that the selective weighting scheme reflects an empirically stable, FDR-supported signal anchored by the cholinergic and dopaminergic systems rather than idiosyncratic single-fit estimates. Of note, human_CHA exhibits high bootstrap stability (89%) despite borderline full-sample FDR significance (and consequently a low spatial correlation with the FDR-anchored NIVA in Fig. 5D), illustrating the complementary information provided by resampling-based stability versus full-sample ranking-controlled significance: a system whose effect is detectable but ranked just past the BH cut-off in the single fit can still be recovered in the majority of bootstrap replicates. Third, voxel-wise correlation between the NIVA and each constituent Hansen PET atlas (Fig. 5D) showed that the composite map preserved the underlying neurochemical spatial patterns of the contributing NT systems while differentially weighting them according to their interaction effect sizes.

The NIVA constitutes a data-driven spatial hypothesis rather than an independently validated biomarker, and its anatomical predictions require prospective confirmation in independent cohorts.

## Small-Lesion Severe-Outcome Phenotype: Neurochemical Mechanism

Among patients with small lesions (TLV < Q1, N = 894), 26.8% had severe outcomes (mRS ≥ 3) at discharge despite minimal structural damage; among the 873 patients with available 12-month follow-up (97.6% retention; 21 lost to follow-up), 158 (18.1%) remained severe at 12 months. Compared to small-lesion patients with good outcomes (mRS ≤ 2), the severe group showed significantly higher cholinergic tract damage (Medial_Path: Cohen's d = 0.268, P = 0.002; Lateral_Path: d = 0.220, P = 0.014) and elevated IL-6 (d ≈ 0.55, P < 0.001; Fig. 6A–B). An additional unexpected observation was a lower α4β2 nicotinic receptor density in the severe group (Cohen's d = −0.64, P < 0.05; Fig. 6A), an isolated reverse-direction signal that warrants independent replication before mechanistic interpretation. These findings suggest that disproportionate cholinergic tract disruption combined with systemic inflammation may contribute to the clinically recognized "small lesion, poor outcome" phenotype. Of note, voxel-wise comparison of lesion distributions between this group and the NIVA hotspots (which were concentrated in deep grey-matter hubs) showed minimal spatial overlap (mean lesion-frequency inside vs. outside NIVA hotspots: 0.06 vs. 1.11), indicating that the severe-outcome small lesions tend to disrupt long-range cholinergic white-matter projections rather than the deep NIVA hubs themselves. A schematic summary integrating these observations is presented in Fig. 6C.

## Domain-Specific Associations (Supplementary Analyses)

Exploratory FDR-corrected OLS analyses across deep phenotyping sub-cohorts identified 17 significant neurochemical–outcome associations (q < 0.05) concentrated in three functional domains (Supplementary Table S1, `Table_S1_Deep_Phenotyping_FDR.csv`). At 6 months, **emotional outcome** (SIS-Emotion, N = 56) was associated with damage to the cholinergic and serotonergic receptor systems (top hits: 5-HT1a, β = −0.47, q = 0.002; CHA, β = −0.61, q = 0.004; 5-HT2a, β = −0.40, q = 0.009; M1, β = −0.42, q = 0.010; JHU_EC, β = −0.41, q = 0.010). At 12 months, **stroke-impact recovery** (SIS_12m_Scale, N = 301) was associated with M1, VAChT, and 5-HT1a damage (all q < 0.05). All other domain comparisons (MoCA, GAD-7, PHQ-9, ESS, PSQI) showed no FDR-significant associations, and the few additional FDR-significant hits at 12 months (Barthel-ADL; Supplementary Table S1) were of small effect size (standardized β ≈ 0.14–0.19; simple Pearson r ≈ 0.10) and inconsistent direction, and are therefore not interpreted further here. Given the modest sub-cohort sizes (particularly for SIS-Emotion, N = 56) and the exploratory nature of this domain-wise analysis, these patterns should be regarded as hypothesis-generating and require replication in larger independent cohorts.

## Supplementary Analyses

Additional analyses are reported in the Supplementary Materials. Pre-synaptic transporter damage (DAT, NAT, 5-HTT, VAChT) showed a larger mean standardized effect size at discharge than post-synaptic receptor damage (mean |β| ≈ 0.18 vs 0.10; Supplementary Fig. S4B). PCA-based system-level aggregation identified the cholinergic system as the strongest predictor at discharge (PC1 OR = 1.18, P = 9.4 × 10⁻⁶; Supplementary Fig. S4A). Separately, significant gradient relationships across outcome strata were observed for all 17 NT loads (Supplementary Fig. S4C). The temporal trajectory of NT effects showed acute predominance, attenuation at 6 months, and partial re-strengthening of associations at 12 months for a subset of pathways (Supplementary Fig. S3). Methodological sensitivity analyses, including 1,000-iteration permutation testing (4 of 17 NT systems remained significant in both parametric and permutation-based tests), mRS cutpoint sensitivity (NAT remained significant across all four dichotomization schemes; A4B2 in 3/4; 5-HT6 in 2/4), and atlas-based spatial-null spin testing (Alexander-Bloch 2018; human_CHA P_spin = 0.001, indicating spatial specificity rather than a generic atlas effect), are detailed in Supplementary Fig. S6. MICE-imputed sensitivity analyses showed concordant directions and effect-size attenuation < 20% relative to complete-case estimates across all primary tests (Methods).
