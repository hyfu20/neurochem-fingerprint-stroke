# Neurotransmitter disconnection unmasks inflammatory vulnerability and shapes functional recovery after ischemic stroke

## Introduction

Stroke is the leading cause of long-term disability worldwide, yet predicting which patients will recover and which will not remains a fundamental clinical challenge. Current prognostic models rely predominantly on macroscopic structural metrics (lesion volume, location, and initial neurological severity), which, while informative, leave a substantial proportion of outcome variance unexplained^1,2. A striking clinical paradox illustrates this gap: patients with near-identical lesion volumes frequently exhibit vastly divergent functional trajectories, suggesting that recovery is governed not merely by *how much* brain tissue is lost, but by *what* that tissue contained at the molecular level.

The past decade has witnessed a paradigm shift from purely anatomical lesion characterization toward network-based approaches. While structural disconnectomics outperforms lesion volume in predicting outcomes^3,4, current models remain molecularly agnostic. By treating all white matter tracts as functionally interchangeable, conventional connectomics ignores the rich chemoarchitectural heterogeneity of the brain's wiring. Anatomically adjacent fibers may carry noradrenergic, serotonergic, or cholinergic projections, each subserving distinct neural computations and, crucially, fundamentally different systemic regulatory functions. This molecular blindness limits our ability to explain the clinical variance observed in structurally similar lesions.

Recent advances in normative neuroimaging have begun to bridge this gap. By integrating large-scale PET tracer maps of neurotransmitter receptors with individual stroke lesion topographies, researchers can now estimate the specific neurochemical systems disrupted by an infarct, an approach termed *neurochemical lesion mapping*^5,6. Koch et al. recently demonstrated the feasibility of this framework, showing that neurotransmitter-informed connectivity predicts motor outcomes beyond structural disconnection alone^7. Building on this intracerebral mapping work, the present study extends neurochemical lesion mapping to the brain's role as a systemic regulator. Specific monoaminergic and cholinergic networks, including the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway, exert tonic top-down regulation of the peripheral immune system^8. We therefore hypothesized that the prognostic impact of these networks is conditional rather than autonomous, becoming clinically consequential primarily under concurrent systemic inflammation, and that the magnitude and direction of this dependence varies across neurotransmitter systems in ways that have not yet been characterized at scale.

To address this gap, we drew on the Third China National Stroke Registry (CNSR-III; N = 15,166)^9 and analysed 3,582 patients with moderate acute ischemic stroke. Within a structural-orthogonalization framework that decouples chemoarchitectural effects from macroscopic tissue loss, we systematically examined two questions across all 17 neurotransmitter systems and two complementary inflammatory markers. First, do specific neurochemical disruptions carry independent prognostic information at 12 months, or does their relevance emerge only in conjunction with systemic inflammation? Second, does the resulting joint dual-burden phenotype, defined by the co-occurrence of focal neurochemical damage and systemic inflammation, provide a parsimonious account for the clinically recognised small-lesion, severe-outcome phenotype?

The result is a neurochemical–inflammatory prognostic atlas in which classical anatomical disruption dominates the acute phase, whereas long-term functional outcome at 12 months is governed by a joint neuro-immune phenotype. Focal neurochemical damage, most consistently to the noradrenergic and cholinergic networks, acquires prognostic relevance primarily when it co-occurs with elevated systemic inflammation. This joint dual-burden phenotype offers a parsimonious account for the small-lesion, severe-outcome presentation and significantly improves risk reclassification beyond standard anatomical models. Individual effect sizes are modest, consistent with a system-level survey rather than single-biomarker discovery, and the framework should accordingly be regarded as a hypothesis-generating atlas to inform biomarker-stratified neuromodulatory and anti-inflammatory trials in stroke recovery, rather than as a standalone predictive tool.
