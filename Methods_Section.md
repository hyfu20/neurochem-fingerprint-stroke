# Methods

## Study Design and Participants

We used data from the Third China National Stroke Registry (CNSR-III), a nationwide, multicenter, prospective, observational registry study of patients with acute ischemic stroke (AIS) or transient ischemic attack enrolled at 201 hospitals in China between August 2015 and March 2018.^9 From this cohort, we identified 3,582 consecutive patients who met the following inclusion criteria: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) AIS confirmed by diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) within 3 days of symptom onset; (3) moderate baseline stroke severity (admission NIHSS 5–15); and (4) available high-quality baseline clinical data with 12-month mRS follow-up (97.2% retention).

The NIHSS range of 5–15 was chosen to capture the most informative clinical window: excluding minor strokes (NIHSS < 5) to avoid the confounding effect of high spontaneous recovery rates, and excluding severe strokes (NIHSS > 15) to minimize the impact of massive, non-specific tissue destruction on chemoarchitectural analysis. Exclusion criteria further included: pre-stroke functional dependence (mRS > 2), a history of major neurological or psychiatric disorders, and imaging artifacts precluding reliable lesion delineation. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Beijing Tiantan Hospital and all participating centers, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their representatives.

## Clinical Assessment and Biomarkers

Baseline neurological severity was quantified at admission using the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS). The primary functional endpoint was 12-month functional status assessed by the modified Rankin Scale (mRS). To capture the temporal evolution of recovery, mRS scores were also collected at discharge, 3 months, and 6 months post-stroke. The mRS was analyzed both as a 7-level ordinal variable and a three-tier categorical outcome: good (0–2), moderate (3–4), and poor (5–6) functional recovery. To move beyond unidimensional functional scales, we performed comprehensive deep phenotyping at 6 and 12 months. Cognitive performance was assessed using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA; total score and sub-domain scores for visuospatial, naming, attention, language, delayed recall, and orientation). Multi-dimensional stroke impact was evaluated via the Stroke Impact Scale (SIS) across eight domains (strength, memory, emotion, communication, ADL, mobility, hand function, social participation). The Barthel Index (BI) quantified activities of daily living independence at baseline and 12 months. Psychological status was screened at baseline using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) for depression and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) for anxiety. For all continuous scales, higher scores consistently represent superior functional or cognitive status, ensuring directional consistency for downstream standardized β coefficient interpretations.

Systemic inflammation was quantified using two complementary baseline serum biomarkers: interleukin-6 (IL-6; available in 73.7% of patients), an upstream pro-inflammatory cytokine, and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP; available in 71.5% of patients), a downstream hepatic acute-phase reactant. Cardiac autonomic function was evaluated via 24-hour Holter electrocardiogram monitoring during the acute phase; the root mean square of successive differences (RMSSD, available in 53%) was extracted as a validated surrogate for parasympathetic (vagal) tone, alongside mean heart rate (HRmean, 55%). Due to their highly skewed biological distributions, IL-6, hsCRP, and RMSSD values were log-transformed (log1p) prior to all statistical modeling to approximate normality and stabilize variance. 

## Neuroimaging Acquisition and Processing

All neuroimaging data were acquired on 3.0-Tesla MRI scanners using a standardized clinical protocol across participating centers. Structural images were obtained using a 2D T1-weighted transverse acquisition (2D-T1w-TRA; typical parameters: TR = 400–600 ms, TE = 8–15 ms, slice thickness = 6 mm with inter-slice gaps, in-plane resolution ≈ 0.9 × 0.9 mm²). DWI sequences were acquired with *b* = 0 and *b* = 1,000 s/mm², and Apparent Diffusion Coefficient (ADC) maps were computed at each site. All neuroimaging preprocessing was performed using the FMRIB Software Library (FSL, version 6.0; https://fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl).

T1-weighted images were processed using FastSurfer (v2.4.2)(Henschel 2020) for whole-brain segmentation into 95 regions (DKT atlas) and cortical surface reconstruction. Subcortical volumes were extracted from the ASEG segmentation. All images were resampled and conformed to 1-mm isotropic resolution (256 × 256 × 256 matrix) as required by the FastSurfer preprocessing pipeline, with the --3T flag enabled to optimize segmentation for 3.0-Tesla acquisitions. Ischemic lesions were automatically segmented using the DeepISLES framework(De La Rosa 2025), a deep-learning-based ensemble algorithm (SEALS) that won the ISLES 2022 challenge, integrating DWI (*b* = 1,000 s/mm²) and ADC maps. All masks were reviewed by two experienced neurologists and manually refined when necessary using ITK-SNAP (v4.0). Total lesion volume (TLV) was calculated in native space.

Given the clinical nature of the structural scans (6 mm slice thickness with inter-slice gaps), nonlinear registration methods were deemed inappropriate. Spatial normalization was achieved via a hierarchical linear registration pipeline using FSL FLIRT. T1-weighted and DWI images were skull-stripped using BET (fractional intensity 0.3, -Z flag for thick-slice optimization). The DWI (*b* = 0) was aligned to T1 via 6-DOF rigid-body transformation with full angular search. To prevent lesion-induced registration bias, cost-function masking (CFM) was applied: the lesion mask was transformed into T1 space, inverted, and supplied as an in-weight, ensuring that transformation matrices were computed exclusively from intact neuroanatomy(Brett 2001). The T1 was then registered to the MNI152 template via 12-DOF affine transformation with CFM. Both matrices were concatenated (convert_xfm) and lesion masks were resampled into MNI space in a single interpolation step using nearest-neighbor interpolation to preserve binary mask properties. All MNI-space registrations were visually quality-controlled in three orthogonal views; cases with poor alignment were excluded. All deep-learning analyses were deployed via Docker containerization on an HPC platform with NVIDIA A100 GPUs.

## Neurotransmitter Atlas Mapping and Neurochemical Fingerprinting

To quantify the neurochemical impact of focal lesions, we adapted the neurotransmitter disconnectomics framework of Koch et al. (Koch 2025) and Alves et al. (Alves 2025). Thirteen PET-derived group-mean density maps of neurotransmitter receptors and transporters were obtained from the Hansen normative atlas (Hansen 2022) via the JuSpace toolbox (Dukart 2021) and projected from cortical grey matter onto white-matter voxels using the Functionnectome framework (Nozais 2021; Alves 2025), which weights each white-matter voxel by the PET density of the cortical regions interconnected through the HCP-1065 streamlines that pass through it. This projection allows subcortical and white-matter lesions to inherit the chemoarchitectural signature of their disconnected cortical targets, rather than the near-zero raw-PET values typical of white matter. Four anatomical cholinergic templates were added: the whole-brain cholinergic terminal-density atlas (CHA; Hansen 2022), the external capsule (JHU_EC; Mori 2008; Hua 2008), and the lateral and medial cholinergic pathways from the Milan probabilistic atlas (Nemy 2020; classification of Selden 1998). The resulting 17 atlases comprise:

- Dopaminergic system: DAT, D1, D2
- Serotonergic system: 5-HTT, 5-HT1a, 5-HT1b, 5-HT2a, 5-HT4, 5-HT6
- Cholinergic system: VAChT, α4β2 (A4B2), M1, and a whole-brain cholinergic terminal density atlas (CHA; Hansen et al., 2022)
- Noradrenergic system: NAT
- Cholinergic white matter tracts: external capsule (JHU_EC), lateral and medial cholinergic pathways

All atlas maps were resampled to 1 mm³ isotropic MNI space prior to computation: continuous receptor/transporter density maps (including the Functionnectome-projected versions) were resampled using trilinear interpolation to preserve quantitative values, whereas binary anatomical masks (cholinergic white-matter tracts and the external-capsule template) were resampled using nearest-neighbor interpolation to maintain tract boundaries.

For each patient, a weighted neurotransmitter lesion load (Load_{k}) was calculated following the Koch et al. (Koch 2025) formulation:

$$Load_{k} = mean_{v \in Lesion}\,Density_{k}(v) \times TLV$$

where $mean_{v \in Lesion}\,Density_{k}(v)$ denotes the mean Functionnectome-projected density of neurotransmitter system $k$ within lesion-affected voxels; for the four anatomical cholinergic templates, which lack a continuous PET-derived density, this quantity reduces to the mean voxel value within the lesion mask. $TLV$ denotes the total lesion volume in mm³. Because the receptor/transporter maps are Functionnectome-projected rather than raw PET, $Load_{k}$ captures both local (grey-matter) and disconnective (white-matter) damage to each chemoarchitectural system within a single closed-form expression. This formulation reflects the disconnectome principle that a white-matter lesion compromises the receptor density of the cortical regions it disconnects. The resulting 17-dimensional load vector constitutes an individual Neurochemical Fingerprint, embedding each patient's damage profile within a normative molecular disconnectomic framework. To decouple neurochemical disruption from classical motor tract disconnection, we quantified physical damage to the corticospinal tract (CST). The bilateral CST template was extracted from the JHU ICBM-DTI-81 white matter atlas (thresholded at 25% maximum probability) and resampled to 1 mm³ MNI space. The physical CST damage load ($V_{CST}$, mm³) was computed as the volumetric intersection between the standardized lesion mask and the bilateral CST template:

$$V_{CST} = Volume(Lesion_{MNI} \cap CST_{bilateral})$$

## Statistical Analysis

**Structural-Functional Decoupling.** A central methodological challenge in lesion-outcome mapping is the extreme collinearity between neurotransmitter loads and total lesion volume (raw r ≈ 0.99, VIF > 700), which conflates chemoarchitectural specificity with gross tissue loss. Following the structural-functional decoupling framework proposed by Koch et al. (2025, *Brain*)(Koch 2025), we orthogonalized each neurotransmitter load against TLV using ordinary least squares (OLS) regression:

$$Resid_{NT,k} = Load_{k} - (\beta_{0} + \beta_{1} \times TLV)$$

The resulting residuals ($Resid_{NT,k}$) isolate the disproportionate neurochemical damage beyond what is expected for a given lesion size (post-residualization VIF ≈ 1). By the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem, these residuals yield unbiased estimates of the independent neurochemical effect with improved numerical stability.

**Ordinal Regression and Hierarchical Sensitivity Analysis.** Associations between neurochemical loading and mRS were modeled using proportional odds ordinal logistic regression, preserving the full ordinal information of the 7-level scale. A hierarchical covariate adjustment strategy tested robustness at progressively stringent confound levels:

- **Model A (Unadjusted)**: mRS ~ Load_NT (raw load, no collinearity issue)
- **Model B (Demographic-adjusted)**: mRS ~ Load_NT + Age + Sex
- **Model C (Fully adjusted)**: mRS ~ Resid_NT + TLV + NIHSS + Age + Sex (Koch residual)
- **Model D (CST-controlled)**: mRS ~ Resid_NT + TLV + NIHSS + CST_Load + Age + Sex

All continuous predictors were z-score standardized prior to model fitting, such that Odds Ratios (OR) reflect the effect per 1-SD increase in the predictor. Models A–C entered the global FDR pool described below; Model D, being a stringent attenuation check after additional CST adjustment, was subjected to local Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction within the 17-NT family (qₙₜ < 0.05) to avoid inflating the global denominator while still controlling the false-discovery rate within this conditional follow-up test. Variables maintaining statistical significance (P < 0.05) across all adjustment levels, particularly those persisting after stringent control for CST disconnection (Model D), were designated as highly robust independent predictors. Adapted from Alves et al.(ref), neurotransmitter variables with a clearly defined synaptic compartment were classified as pre-synaptic (transporters: DAT, NAT, 5-HTT, VAChT) or post-synaptic (receptors: A4B2, M1, 5-HT1a, 5-HT1b, 5-HT2a, 5-HT4, 5-HT6, D1, D2). The composite cholinergic terminal-density atlas (CHA) and three white-matter tract masks (JHU_EC, lateral and medial cholinergic pathways) were excluded from this dichotomy because they index broad axonal projections rather than a single synaptic compartment, leaving 13 of 17 NT systems for the pre/post-synaptic comparison. Given the small number of variables per group, between-group differences in absolute effect sizes (|β|) were tested using the Mann–Whitney U test. To characterize system-level vulnerability while avoiding cancellation of opposing effects within the same neurotransmitter family (e.g., inhibitory 5-HT1a vs. excitatory 5-HT2a), we applied principal component analysis (PCA) to extract the first principal component (PC1) for each of the five neurotransmitter systems. PC1 direction was aligned with total system load (i.e., sign-flipped when the Pearson correlation between PC1 and the sum of constituent loads was negative) to ensure that higher PC1 values correspond to greater damage. System-level PC1 scores were then entered into ordinal logistic regression models with the same covariate structure as Model C. To establish neurochemical specificity, 1,000 label-permutation spin tests (Koch 2025; Alexander-Bloch 2018) were performed: lesion masks were held constant while NT atlas labels were randomly rotated in spherical space. Findings were considered chemically specific only if the observed effect exceeded the spatially permuted null distribution (one-tailed P_spin < 0.05).

**Neuro-Immune Vulnerability Atlas (NIVA).** To generate a spatially explicit synthesis of neuro-immune vulnerability, we constructed a composite brain map, termed the NIVA, by projecting 14 PET-derived neurotransmitter receptor/transporter density maps (Hansen et al., 2022; excluding 3 binary white matter tract masks that lack voxel-wise density information) onto MNI152 space, each weighted by its empirical NT × inflammation interaction effect size as −log₁₀(global FDR q) (capped at 5). To enforce statistical selectivity, NT systems that did not survive the global FDR threshold (q ≥ 0.05) were assigned zero weight and therefore did not contribute to the composite, ensuring that NIVA reflects only NT systems with empirically supported neuro-immune interactions (6 of 14 NT systems contributed in the present cohort). The composite map was thresholded at the 80th percentile to delineate regions of maximal neuro-immune convergence. Robustness was assessed via three complementary analyses, deliberately calibrating the statistical stringency to the data regime in which each was evaluated: spatial split-half cross-validation (raw P < 0.05 per half, matched to the bootstrap criterion so that both resampling analyses share a common per-replicate threshold and remain powered at n ≈ 1,800), 1,000-iteration bootstrap resampling of NT weights at raw P < 0.05 per replicate followed by selection-frequency stability (Hansen et al., 2022), and voxel-wise correlation with constituent PET atlases. The full-sample anchor map (Fig. 5A) retained the strict BH-FDR gating; the relaxed gating schemes were used only to characterize stability under resampling.

**Mediation sensitivity analysis.** As a sensitivity check on the effect-modification interpretation of the NT × IL-6 interactions, we performed bootstrap mediation analysis (5,000 iterations) for the single pathway Resid_NT → log1p(IL-6) → mRS, with TLV, NIHSS, Age, and Sex as covariates. The indirect effect (a × b) was estimated using percentile bootstrap 95% confidence intervals. Prior to mediation analysis, missing-data bias was assessed by comparing baseline characteristics (Age, Sex, NIHSS, TLV, mRS, IL-6) between patients with and without IL-6 measurements using Mann–Whitney U and chi-squared tests.

**Dynamic inflammation trajectory sub-cohort analysis.** As a longitudinal sensitivity check on the cross-sectional CHA × IL-6 / CHA × hsCRP interactions, we leveraged the CNSR-III protocol-defined hsCRP repeat measurements at 3 months (M03) and 12 months (M12) post-stroke. IL-6 was protocol-defined as a baseline-only measurement and could not be used for trajectory analyses. The longitudinal sub-cohort comprised 1,334 patients with paired baseline + 3-month hsCRP and 12-month mRS (full three-time-point sub-cohort N = 820). We computed a per-month log-hsCRP resolution slope as [log1p(M03_hsCRP) − log1p(BSL_hsCRP)] / 3, and defined a "sustained-inflammation" phenotype as M03_hsCRP ≥ 3 mg/L (Ridker AHA/CDC clinical threshold). To test the *a priori* hypothesis that cholinergic disconnection impairs the resolution of systemic inflammation, we (i) compared the slope distribution across Resid_human_CHA tertiles (Kruskal–Wallis + Spearman ρ + covariate-adjusted OLS); (ii) modelled the dynamic interaction in a fully adjusted ordinal logistic regression of 12-month mRS on z(Resid_CHA) × z(slope) + z(log1p BSL_hsCRP) + TLV + NIHSS + Age + Sex, with the interaction term assessed by a 1-degree-of-freedom likelihood-ratio test against the main-effects nested model; and (iii) applied the same four-cell additive-interaction framework used for Fig. 3B to the dichotomous Resid_CHA × sustained-hsCRP phenotype, with RERI, AP and Synergy index 95% CIs obtained from 1,000-iteration patient-level bootstrap resampling. As a pre-specified sensitivity analysis to address the concern that the BSL → M03 slope window may not capture late inflammation resolution, the three primary inferential analyses (slope ~ CHA, ordinal dynamic interaction, four-cell additive interaction) were repeated on the three-time-point sub-cohort (N = 820) using the BSL → M12 per-month log-slope, [log1p(M12_hsCRP) − log1p(BSL_hsCRP)] / 12, in place of the BSL → M03 slope, with the M12 sensitivity estimates written to a parallel output directory (out/dynamic_inflammation_triplet/) and reported alongside the main-analysis estimates. Results are reported in Supplementary Fig. S7 and the dynamic-trajectory output tables.

**Small-Lesion Phenotype and Symptom Mapping.** To investigate why some patients with small lesions (TLV < 25th percentile, ≤ 0.9 mL) experience disproportionately poor outcomes (mRS ≥ 3), we compared the "small-lesion severe" subgroup against "small-lesion good" (mRS ≤ 2) controls on: (1) baseline characteristics; (2) Resid_NT values for all 17 pathways (Mann–Whitney U + Cohen's d); (3) RMSSD and IL-6 levels; and (4) within-subgroup ordinal regression (excluding TLV from covariates to avoid restriction-of-range bias). To investigate whether specific neurotransmitter systems exert domain-selective effects (i.e., preferential associations between specific NT systems and specific functional domains), we tested associations between Resid_NT and multi-dimensional behavioral outcomes (MoCA total and sub-scores, SIS sub-domains, Barthel Index, PHQ-9, GAD-7) using OLS regression with comprehensive covariate adjustment (TLV, NIHSS, Age, Sex, and CST_Load). Both predictors and continuous outcomes were z-score standardized to yield standardized β coefficients, allowing for direct cross-domain comparison of neurochemical impact magnitude. FDR correction (Benjamini–Hochberg, q < 0.05) was applied within each outcome domain to preserve sensitivity for detecting domain-specific effects.

Deep phenotyping assessments were available in sub-cohorts (N = 56–433); non-responder analyses confirmed no systematic attrition bias (all P > 0.05). To test whether systemic inflammation modifies the prognostic impact of focal neurochemical damage, we modeled interaction terms in fully adjusted ordinal models: mRS ~ Resid_NT + log1p(IL-6) + Resid_NT × log1p(IL-6) + TLV + NIHSS + Age + Sex, retaining both main effects alongside the interaction term. Following the dual-scale framework recommended by Knol & VanderWeele (2012), the dichotomous dual-burden phenotype (CHA × IL-6) was further evaluated on both the multiplicative and the additive scale: (i) on the multiplicative scale, a 1-degree-of-freedom likelihood-ratio test compared a logistic model with the cross-product term against a main-effects-only nested model (covariates: TLV, NIHSS, Age, Sex); (ii) on the additive scale, the four median-split cells (LL [reference], LH, HL, HH) were entered as three dummy variables in a single covariate-adjusted logistic regression, from which the Relative Excess Risk due to Interaction (RERI = OR_HH − OR_LH − OR_HL + 1), the Attributable Proportion (AP = RERI / OR_HH) and the Synergy Index (S = (OR_HH − 1) / [(OR_LH − 1) + (OR_HL − 1)]) were derived, with 95% confidence intervals obtained from 1,000-iteration patient-level bootstrap resampling and refit. As a complementary visualization on the continuous inflammation scale, covariate-adjusted simple-slope models were fit within each tertile of Resid_human_CHA: 12-month mRS was regressed on z-scored baseline IL-6 by ordinary least squares with adjustment for TLV, NIHSS, Age and Sex, separately within the low, mid and high CHA-damage tertiles (per-tertile slopes and P-values reported in Table_S5_SimpleSlope_CHA_IL6.csv). Threshold effects were assessed by dividing raw neurotransmitter loads into quartiles. Kruskal–Wallis tests assessed overall differences in mRS across quartiles, and Spearman rank correlation quantified monotonic trends. Ordinal logistic regression compared the highest quartile (Q4) against the combined lower quartiles (Q1–Q3) to identify clinically meaningful thresholds.

**Validation and Sensitivity Analyses.** The cohort was partitioned into Discovery (70%, *N* = 2,507) and Validation (30%, *N* = 1,075) sets, stratified by mRS. Incremental predictive value was assessed via 10-fold cross-validation (AUC for mRS > 2 vs. ≤ 2). Decision Curve Analysis quantified clinical net benefit across threshold probabilities 0.05–0.95(Vickers & Elkin 2006). Sensitivity analyses were performed using four alternative mRS groupings: (A) 0–1 vs. 2–6; (B) 0–2 vs. 3–6; (C) 0–3 vs. 4–6; and (D) the primary three-group ordinal scheme. Non-responder bias analyses (Mann–Whitney U and chi-squared tests) confirmed that missingness in Holter-derived (RMSSD, HRmean) and deep phenotyping sub-cohorts (MoCA sub-scores, SIS sub-domains) satisfied the missing-at-random (MAR) assumption, with no significant baseline clinical differences (Age, Sex, NIHSS, TLV, mRS) between patients with and without each assessment (all P > 0.05). Primary mechanism discovery analyses were conducted using complete cases. As a sensitivity analysis, missing baseline covariates were imputed using Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE; m = 5 imputed datasets, 20 iterations per dataset, 10 nearest features, posterior sampling). Logarithmic transformations were applied to highly skewed variables (e.g., IL-6, RMSSD) prior to imputation to preserve biological distributions. Consistency was confirmed if ΔOR between complete-case and pooled imputed models was < 20% with concordant significance direction (pre-specified threshold). To protect against Type I error inflation across this comprehensive analytical pipeline, a Global False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction was performed using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. All P-values generated across the regression, dose-response, interaction, mediation, system-level PCA, permutation, recurrence, spin-test, WMH-interaction, and anomalous-recovery modules (total tests: 390) were pooled into a single global correction array. Statistical significance was defined as two-sided P < 0.05 or Global FDR q < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using Python 3.10 (statsmodels v0.14, scikit-learn v1.3, SciPy v1.11). Structural neuroimaging analysis was performed using FastSurfer (v2.4.2)(Henschel 2020) under FreeSurfer license (v7.3.2). Lesion segmentation was performed using DeepISLES(De La Rosa 2025) with the SEALS ensemble algorithm. Spatial normalization and atlas computations were performed entirely within FSL (v6.0; FLIRT, convert_xfm, applywarp, fslstats). Neurotransmitter atlas maps were sourced from JuSpace(Dukart 2021) and the Hansen et al. normative PET atlas(Hansen 2022). All processing was containerized via Docker for reproducibility.
